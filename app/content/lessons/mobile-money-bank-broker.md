## Content

Most investing guides are written assuming you already have a bank account
and a credit card. In Rwanda, that's backwards: mobile money reaches far
more people than traditional banking does. As of Rwanda's 2024 FinScope
survey, about 86% of adults use mobile money, while only around 22% are
"banked" in the traditional sense (NISR, 2024). So where does investing fit
in?

**Mobile money** (like MTN MoMo or Airtel Money) is how most Rwandans move
money day to day. It's excellent for small, frequent transactions — but on
its own, a mobile money wallet doesn't buy you shares or units in a fund.

**A bank account** is still useful for larger or recurring transfers — for
example, some investment products require funding above a mobile-money
transaction limit, or use a bank standing order for recurring contributions.
RNIT's Iterambere Fund, for instance, explicitly supports a systematic
investment plan funded "either through their respective employer or through
a bank standing order" (RNIT, 2024) — but its minimum entry amount (RWF
2,000) is low enough that many people fund their first contribution directly
via mobile money or an agent instead.

**A licensed broker or fund manager** is who you actually invest *through*.
They are registered with Rwanda's Capital Markets Authority (CMA), and they
are the entity that executes a trade or processes your unit purchase. Tera
doesn't do this — we point you to institutions that do, in Lesson 4.

The practical takeaway: you likely already have the payment method (mobile
money) you need to fund your first investment. What's usually missing isn't
money or a bank account — it's knowing *which* licensed institution to send
that money to, and that's what the rest of this course covers.
