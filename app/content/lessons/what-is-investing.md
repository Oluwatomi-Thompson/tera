## Content

If you have a mobile money wallet, you're already "financially included" — and
in Rwanda, most people are. Mobile money reaches roughly 86% of adults
nationally, part of a 96% overall financial inclusion rate (NISR, 2024).

But financial inclusion isn't the same as investing. **Saving** means putting
money aside — in a mobile money wallet, a bank account, or an *ikimina*
(informal savings group) — and getting back roughly what you put in.
**Investing** means putting money into something (shares in a company, a
government bond, a professionally managed fund) with the expectation that it
grows in value over time — and the real possibility that it could also lose
value. That trade-off, more potential growth for more risk, is the core idea
behind every lesson in Tera.

A 2026 study of 300 young Rwandan adults found that more than 90% already use
a formal financial service and more than 85% use mobile money — but
participants showed only modest knowledge of core financial concepts, with
the gap wider among women and less-educated respondents (Ntakirutimana et
al., 2026). In other words: access to financial tools is not the problem.
Knowing what to do with them is. That's the gap Tera exists to close.

**Why this matters now:** money sitting still loses value every year to
inflation. Money invested, even modestly, has a chance to grow faster than
that. The rest of this course walks through how, specifically, that works in
Rwanda — not in a US brokerage account you'll never open.
